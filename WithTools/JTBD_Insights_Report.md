# Student Learning Report: Understanding Clayton Christensen’s Jobs-To-Be-Done (JTBD) Framework

**To:** Undergraduate Entrepreneurship Students  
**From:** Entrepreneurship Report Writer  
**Subject:** Demystifying Customer Needs Through the Jobs-To-Be-Done Framework  

---

## 1. A Simple Definition of JTBD

When you launch a startup, a common piece of advice you will hear is: *"Build something customers want."* But how do you actually figure out what they want? Traditional market research often points you toward demographics—asking about age, income, and lifestyle—or asks customers what features they want added to a product. 

Clayton Christensen’s **Jobs-To-Be-Done (JTBD)** framework turns that traditional thinking upside down. 

> **The Core Idea:** Customers don't simply buy products or services; they **"hire"** them to make progress in a specific circumstance. 

Think of it this way: People have fundamental tasks, problems, or "jobs" that arise in their daily lives. When a job arises, they go out into the market and look for a product to hire. If your product does that job better, faster, or more cheaply than the alternatives, they buy it. If it doesn't, they don't. 

---

## 2. Main Insights from the Research

The research highlights several critical takeaways for innovators and entrepreneurs:

* **Demographics don't buy products, circumstances do:** Knowing that a buyer is a 28-year-old male earning $60,000 a year doesn't tell you *why* he is purchasing your product right now. Knowing his **circumstance** (e.g., he is stuck in rush-hour traffic and starving) explains everything.
* **Avoid the Feature Trap:** Asking focus groups what features they want (e.g., "Do you want more chocolate in your milkshake?") often leads to dead ends. Instead of asking what people want *in* a product, focus on the **struggle** they are trying to resolve.
* **Your real competitors are surprises:** Your product isn't just competing against direct industry rivals. It is competing against *anything* else the customer might hire to get that same job done.

## 3. Helping You Understand Customer Jobs: The Milkshake Story & Examples

### The Classic Example: The McDonald’s Milkshake
McDonald’s wanted to sell more milkshakes. They did traditional research: brought in demographic groups, asked them what they wanted in a milkshake (more chocolate flavor, more thickness), and made those upgrades. **Result:** Sales didn't budge an inch.

Researchers then spent 18 hours observing what was actually happening in the restaurants. They discovered a shocking pattern:
* **The Pattern:** About half of all milkshakes were sold before 8:30 AM.
* **The Buyer:** The customer was always alone, bought nothing else, got straight into a car, and drove away.
* **The "Job":** When interviewed, these morning commuters explained that they had a long, boring drive to work. They needed something to keep them awake and engaged during the commute, and they needed to stay full until 10:00 AM. 
* **The Competitors:** What did they hire previously for this job? **Donuts** (too messy), **bagels** (too dry, required dangerous steering-wheel balancing with cream cheese), **Snickers bars** (caused guilt), and **bananas** (eaten in under a minute, didn't last). 
* **Why the Milkshake Won:** The milkshake was thick, took 23 minutes to suck through a straw, kept the driver entertained, fit in a cup holder, and kept hunger at bay until 10:00 AM. McDonald's wasn't selling a dessert; they were selling a morning commute companion!

> 🎨 **Illustrative Example (Created for Students): The Saturday Morning Parenting Kit**  
> Imagine an entrepreneur trying to sell a new tablet pre-loaded with educational toddler games. If they look at demographics, they target "parents of kids aged 2–4." But applying JTBD reveals the real job: *“Give me 45 minutes of quiet time to drink my coffee hot on a Saturday morning without feeling guilty about screen time.”* The tablet isn't just competing with other educational apps; it’s competing with a pile of wooden blocks, a box of cereal, or letting the toddler watch cartoons.

## 4. How JTBD Helps Entrepreneurs Discover Customer Needs

For an entrepreneur building a new venture, JTBD is a powerful lens for discovery:

1. **Shift Focus from Product to Progress:** Stop thinking about how cool your technology or product features are. Start thinking about the *progress* your customer is trying to make in their life.
2. **Uncover Hidden Pain Points:** By studying the exact circumstances surrounding a customer's struggle, you discover emotional and physical constraints (e.g., *“I need to eat this while driving with one hand”* or *“I need to feel professional without spending a fortune”*).
3. **Build Better Solutions:** When you clearly define the job, you can design a product that hits every single requirement of that specific situation, ensuring a high product-market fit right out of the gate.

---

## 5. How JTBD Connects to the Value Proposition Canvas

In entrepreneurship courses, you will often use the **Value Proposition Canvas (VPC)** to ensure your product matches customer needs. JTBD forms the beating heart of that canvas:

* **The Customer Profile (Right Side of VPC):** 
  * **Customer Jobs:** This is literally the JTBD framework! What functional, social, and emotional jobs is the customer trying to get done?
  * **Pains:** What annoyances, obstacles, or risks do they face while trying to get that job done? (e.g., donuts being messy, bagels being dry).
  * **Gains:** What outcomes or benefits are they hoping to achieve?
* **The Value Map (Left Side of VPC):**
  * **Products & Services:** What are you offering to help them? (e.g., a viscous breakfast drink).
  * **Pain Relievers:** How does your product eliminate their struggles? (e.g., fits in a cup holder, doesn't spill).
  * **Gain Creators:** How does your product produce outcomes? (e.g., keeps you full for hours).

**In short:** JTBD defines *why* the customer is on the right side of the canvas, allowing you to design the precise solution for the left side.

## 6. Three Questions to Ask Your Customer Segments

When you go out to interview potential users for your own startup project, ditch the generic surveys and ask these three JTBD-inspired questions:

1. **"Take me back to the exact day/moment you decided you needed to buy [Product Category]. What happened in your life that morning?"**  
   *(This uncovers the specific **circumstance** and trigger event.)*
2. **"Before you found our solution, what did you hire to do this job, and why did it frustrate you?"**  
   *(This reveals your **real competitors**—which might not be companies in your industry—and the hidden pain points.)*
3. **"When you use our product successfully, what does it allow you to stop worrying about?"**  
   *(This digs into the deeper **emotional and social job** the customer is trying to accomplish.)*
